# Several reviewers, strand B: decision rights, and judgment by several people

Provenance: one of three research strands run on 2026-11-11 for [[Several Reviewers]] (P20), by an agent working read-only from the web; the synthesis is [[Several Reviewers - Research]]. The questions it answers are that note's Q1 to Q8. Study abstracts were read as raw text from PubMed, OpenAlex or arXiv, so their numbers are as published. **Web pages (RFCs, handbook chapters, governance pages) were read through a fetch that summarises, so quoted wording should be checked against the page before it's repeated anywhere public.**

## Findings

### A. Who decides: frameworks, as their sources specify them

1. **RACI** separates who does the work (Responsible) from the one who answers for it (Accountable); Consulted is two-way, Informed one-way. "Some theories of project management" require exactly one Accountable per task. Source: Wikipedia, [Responsibility assignment matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsibility_assignment_matrix), citing the PMBOK; no primary specification found. A practitioner framework. Q2, Q5, Q7: one named decider per card, and "your answer is wanted" and "for your information" are different kinds of inbox.
2. **DACI.** The Approver is "the one person (yes: one!) who makes the decision". Contributors have a voice and no vote; Informed have neither. Options go in a table with pros and cons, and the decision is written down. Source: Atlassian, [DACI](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci). Practitioner framework. Q2, Q3, Q7.
3. **RAPID (Bain).** One Recommender. Agree roles are "assigned sparingly", and their input must be reflected in the proposal. "Ideally, there should only be one decider", who "has primary accountability for the outcome". If Recommend and Agree can't reconcile, "the decide role will make the final call". If a group holds the D, there must be "clarity up front on how they will reach a final decision". Source: Bain, [RAPID Decision Making](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-decision-making/). Practitioner framework. Q1, Q2, Q3, Q5: the agent is the Recommender; an expert can hold Agree on some rows; a tie-break is fixed in advance wherever more than one decides.
4. **Amazon's one-way and two-way doors.** Irreversible decisions get slow, consultative handling; reversible ones "can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups". The heavy process on reversible decisions produces "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion". Source: Amazon's [2015 letter to shareholders](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312516530910/d168744dex991.htm). Practitioner statement. Q1, Q2, Q6: how many answer should follow reversibility, which a card's level already encodes.
5. **Disagree and commit.** Dissent is stated, then the person commits. True misalignment is escalated quickly, because "you've worn me down" is an awful process. Sources: Amazon's [2016 letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312517120198/d373368dex991.htm), its [leadership principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles). Practitioner statement. Q3, Q6: disagreement ends by escalation to a named person, not by more rounds.
6. **The advice process.** "Any person can make any decision after seeking advice from" those meaningfully affected and those with expertise. Advice doesn't bind, and "there is no power to block". "The bigger the decision, the wider the net needs to be cast". Source: the Reinventing Organizations wiki, [Decision making](https://reinventingorganizationswiki.com/en/theory/decision-making/). Practitioner framework. Q1, Q2, Q4: an alternative to a quorum is one decider who must have collected named people's input.
7. **Consent, not consensus (Sociocracy 3.0).** A proposal passes when nobody has an objection: a reasoned argument showing harm, or a worthwhile improvement. A "concern" can't be backed by reasoning; it doesn't block, and is recorded. Decisions are "good enough for now and safe enough to try until the next review". Each person responds in a round "without responding to others". Source: [Consent decision making](https://patterns.sociocracy30.org/consent-decision-making.html). Practitioner framework. Q2, Q3, Q4: two grades of dissent, and a review date.
8. **IETF rough consensus.** The chair judges it. "Lack of disagreement is more important than agreement." Issues must be "addressed, but not necessarily accommodated". Counts don't decide: 100 in favour with one unaddressed issue isn't consensus, and 5 for with 100 against can be. An objector is owed a reasoned explanation and can appeal, up a ladder, within two months. Sources: [RFC 7282](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7282.html), [RFC 2026](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2026.html) section 6.5. Normative. Q2, Q3: don't count votes; a named judge says why an objection was dismissed; an appeal has a ladder and a limit.
9. **Apache's voting.** Only PMC votes bind, and the rule differs by kind of decision: a code change needs three +1, and a -1 is a veto that "cannot be overruled nor overridden by anyone", though "a veto without a justification is invalid"; procedural votes go by majority; a release needs three +1 and "may not be vetoed". Votes run at least 72 hours; lazy consensus is "silence gives assent". Sources: [Apache voting](https://www.apache.org/foundation/voting.html), [decision making](https://community.apache.org/committers/decisionMaking.html). Normative. Q2, Q3, Q6: different rows get different rules; a block needs a reason; silence as assent always has a clock.
10. **Rust's RFCs.** "A sub-team makes final decisions." A member proposes a final comment period of ten calendar days with a disposition. The README says all members sign off; the bot's code starts the period once a majority has reviewed, fewer than three reviews are outstanding and no named concern is open. Concerns are named, and only their author can resolve them. Sources: [the RFC README](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/blob/master/README.md), [rfcbot](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcbot-rs). Normative, and its implementation. Q2, Q3, Q6.
11. **Python's PEP-Delegate.** The Steering Council has final authority; a core developer may volunteer as a PEP's delegate, with "the authority to approve or reject that PEP"; anyone may ask the Council to review the delegation. The author is responsible for "documenting dissenting opinions". Source: [PEP 1](https://peps.python.org/pep-0001/). Normative. Q1, Q2, Q3, Q5: a decider per decision, chosen for expertise, under a standing body that can take it back.
12. **Kubernetes OWNERS.** Reviewers and approvers are listed per directory; a bot suggests "at least two" reviewers and one approver. Unresponsive people are unassigned, which the page calls a sign of stale OWNERS files. Roles are a counted ladder (three months as a member and primary reviewer on five pull requests, for a reviewer); role changes are pull requests approved by existing approvers; inactive people are pruned. Sources: [OWNERS files](https://www.kubernetes.dev/docs/guide/owners/), [community membership](https://github.com/kubernetes/community/blob/master/community-membership.md). Normative. Q1, Q2, Q5, Q6, Q8.
13. **Review tooling.** Gerrit: +1 is "looks good to me, but someone else must approve"; "any +2 enables submit"; "any -2 blocks submit". GitHub's team assignment removes the team and assigns named members, "to reduce noise for your team and clarify individual responsibility". Sources as in [[Several Reviewers - Strand A Review Tools]]. Q1, Q2, Q6, Q7.
14. **Architecture decision records.** A reversed decision is kept, "marked as superseded"; the record lists all consequences, "not just the 'positive' ones"; it spares newcomers "blind acceptance or blind reversal". A rejected one is kept with its reason "to prevent future discussions on the same topic". Sources: Nygard, [Documenting architecture decisions](https://www.cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions); [AWS's ADR process](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/architectural-decision-records/adr-process.html). Practitioner. Q3, Q8.
15. **Latency norms.** Google: "one business day is the maximum time it should take to respond to a code review request", and a busy reviewer says when, or suggests someone else. Google SRE: primary and secondary on-call with fall-through, and "clear escalation paths". Sources: [review speed](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/speed.html), [Being on-call](https://sre.google/sre-book/being-on-call/). Practitioner. Q6, Q7: a stated response time, a named fallback, a cap on each person's load.

### B. Judgment by several people

16. **Delphi** questions experts one by one in rounds "interspersed with controlled opinion feedback". In a review, Delphi groups beat statistical groups in 12 studies to 2, and ordinary interacting groups in 5 to 1; no consistent evidence that it beats other structured procedures. Sources: [Dalkey and Helmer 1963](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.9.3.458); [Rowe and Wright 1999](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0169-2070(99)00018-7). A narrative review of experiments. Q4: answer alone first, then controlled feedback.
17. **Social influence.** Showing people others' estimates (N = 144) narrowed the spread of opinion "without improvements of its collective error", and raised confidence. In a music market with 14,341 participants, seeing earlier choices made outcomes more unequal and less predictable. In a cascade it can be optimal to follow one's predecessors "without regard to his own information". Sources: [Lorenz et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21576485/); [Salganik et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16469928/); [Bikhchandani et al. 1992](https://doi.org/10.1086/261849). Experiments and theory. Q4, Q7: don't show counts or earlier answers before a person commits.
18. **Herding after independent opinions.** A randomised trial at a machine learning conference (1,544 papers, 2,797 reviewers) found "no evidence of herding" from the first argument in discussion, where each reviewer had formed an opinion first. Source: [Stelmakh et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37437010/). An RCT, one setting. Q3, Q4: once each has committed, open discussion didn't add arbitrariness.
19. **Discussion and reliability.** Two panels scored the same 65 grant proposals: agreement between them was kappa 0.23 after discussion and 0.23 from the plain mean of scores, so "panel discussions per se did not improve the reliability". In constructed NIH panels, reviewers within a panel agreed more after discussion, and different panels less. Sources: [Fogelholm et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21831594/); [Pier et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/reseval/rvw025). Q3: discussion ends a disagreement; it doesn't show the result is right.
20. **Noise** is "undesirable variability in judgments of the same problem". In the book's insurance audit, underwriters' premiums for the same cases differed by a median 55%, about five times what executives expected. Its remedies include aggregating independent judgments, structured processes, rules and noise audits. Source: read only through Wikipedia's [summary of the book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise:_A_Flaw_in_Human_Judgment). Secondary. Q2, Q8: with several reviewers, which person a card reaches becomes a source of variation worth measuring.
21. **Experts agree less than expected.** Ten radiologists reading 150 mammograms: weighted kappa 0.47. Among 43 NIH reviewers of 25 applications, "no agreement": the outcome "depended more on the reviewer to whom the grant was assigned than the research proposed". Percent agreement ignores chance. Sources: [Elmore et al. 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7969300/); [Pier et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29507248/); [McHugh 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23092060/). Observational, and a methods paper. Q3, Q8. The strand's own arithmetic: with 50% agreement by chance on a two-option card, this project's 59% blind agreement with its agent is a kappa of about 0.18. Two human reviewers should be expected to differ often, so the path for disagreement is a main path.
22. **When two heads beat one.** For two observers of similar sensitivity who could talk, two were better than one; for very different sensitivities, "two heads were actually worse than the better one". Taking the more confident member's answer reproduces the gain, but fails when most are wrong, since confidence tracks consensus, not accuracy. Sources: [Bahrami et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20798320/); [Koriat 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22517862/). Lab experiments on perception. Q1, Q2, Q8: pair reviewers of comparable competence in the card's area; don't settle a disagreement by confidence.
23. **Cochrane's rules.** For study eligibility it's mandatory to "use (at least) two people working independently" and to "define in advance the process for resolving disagreements"; at the title and abstract stage one person is acceptable. Two independent extractors are mandatory for outcome data. Most disagreements are simple errors settled by discussion; arbitration by another person is needed "more rarely". The originally extracted data are kept beside the consensus. A non-expert second screener "may be an advantage". Sources: the Cochrane Handbook, [chapter 4](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04), [chapter 5](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-05). Normative, citing evidence. Q1 to Q4: double only the consequential step.
24. **Single against dual screening.** A crowd RCT (280 participants, 24,942 decisions): single screening missed 13% of relevant studies, dual 3%, with specificity down from 79% to 69%. Edwards: single reviewers missed 8% on average (0 to 24), pairs none. A methodological review: a median 5% missed, 3% for experienced reviewers and 13% for the less experienced. Across 329,332 decisions by 86 reviewers, a single reviewer's error rate was 10.76%. Sources: [Gartlehner et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31972274/); [Edwards et al. 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12111924/); [Waffenschmidt et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31253092/); [Wang et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31935267/). One RCT, reviews, observational studies. Q2, Q8: a second independent answer cuts misses from about one in ten to a few in a hundred, with more false alarms; an experienced single reviewer comes close to a pair.
25. **Double data extraction.** One extractor with a verifier made more errors than two independent extractors (a relative difference of 21.7%, P = .019) and took 36% less time. Sources: [Buscemi et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16765272/); [Mathes et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29179685/). A pilot randomised study; a review of six. Q2, Q4: "a second person checks the first's answer" is weaker than two independent answers, and cheaper.
26. **Double reading in mammography.** Across 17 studies, double reading with arbitration raised detection and lowered recall. For digital mammography (4 studies) the differences weren't significant, and the authors call evidence of benefit "scarce". Across diagnostic radiology, the authors suggest double reading only "for selected, high-risk examination types". Sources: [Taylor and Potts 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18353630/); [Posso et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29103474/); [Geijer and Geijer 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29594850/). Systematic reviews. Q1, Q2: two answers on high-stakes rows only; the benefit depends on how the two are combined.
27. **Blinding the second reader.** In 1,119,191 screens across 43 English centres, an unblinded second reader was likelier to recall when the first had; blinding went with fewer recalls (odds ratio 0.923), no clear change in detection, and a higher predictive value. Possible confounding by centre. A policy review: effectiveness "is contingent on whether the two radiologists are blinded to one another's decisions, and how the decisions of the two radiologists are integrated". Sources: [Cooper et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34117912/); [Taylor-Phillips and Stinton 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31617741/), abstract only. A large cohort; a narrative review. Q4: an unblinded second answer agrees its way past the arbiter.
28. **Arbitration in practice.** In the NHS programme, "double reading of mammograms by 2 readers is mandatory"; discordant reads go to a consensus panel or a third reader, who must meet thresholds of volume and experience and be approved. Readers are audited yearly against peers. "Ideally inexperienced readers should be paired with experienced readers." A scoping review found "a lack of guidance and underpinning evidence" on how best to arbitrate. Sources: [NHS guidance for image reading](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/breast-screening-guidance-for-image-reading/breast-screening-guidance-for-image-reading); [Hackney et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28390551/). Normative; a scoping review. Q3, Q5, Q8: the arbiter is a qualified, appointed role; new reviewers are paired and audited.
29. **Doubling by risk.** In the MASAI trial (80,033 women), an AI risk score sent examinations to single reading or, at the top score, double reading. Detection was 6.1 against 5.1 per 1,000 with standard double reading (p = 0.052), false positives 1.5% in both arms, and reading workload fell 44%. An interim safety analysis. Source: [Lång et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37541274/). An RCT, interim. Q1, Q2: a gate that decides how many people see an item can keep accuracy while roughly halving the load.
30. **Mandated double-checks decay.** A systematic review (13 studies) found "insufficient evidence" that double-checking medication lowers errors. In 5,140 observed administrations, mandated checks were independent in 1.0% of cases and "primed" in 92.5%, showed no association with errors, and cost 6.4 minutes each. Interviews named "deference to authority, reduction of responsibility, automatic processing and lack of time". Sources: [Koyama et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31391315/); [Westbrook et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32769177/); [Armitage 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19133335/). Q2, Q4, Q6: a second answer that isn't independent by construction buys nothing.
31. **Checking in sequence.** In two lab experiments, a blinded second checker showed no loafing, and the team was near perfect; a second checker who saw the first's evaluation cut their effort substantially. In another, people told that a second person was monitoring in parallel cross-checked the automation significantly less. Sources: [Cymek and Manzey 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35786941/); [Domeinski et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1177/154193120705101004). Lab experiments. Q4, Q6, Q7: hide the first answer, and consider not telling a reviewer that a second person holds the same card.
32. **Two-person rules guard against insiders.** NIST: "enforce dual authorization" for defined privileged actions, aimed at insider threat, with an exception where "immediate responses are necessary". In a lab corruption game, two officials deciding jointly increased corrupt behaviour. Sources: NIST SP 800-53 AC-3(2), read at [a mirror](https://csf.tools/reference/nist-sp-800-53/r5/ac/ac-3/ac-3-2/); [Schickora 2011](https://ideas.repec.org/p/lmu/muenec/12160.html). A normative control; one lab experiment. Q2, Q5: a two-person rule on hard cards or on changes to the preferences is a control against one account acting alone; it needs separate decisions, not a joint one.
33. **Social loafing.** A meta-analysis of 78 studies: people put in less effort on collective than on individual tasks; the strongest moderators were "evaluation potential, expectations of co-worker performance, task meaningfulness, and culture". Source: [Karau and Williams 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.65.4.681). Q6, Q8: each person's answers must be visible and evaluated as theirs; pooled credit invites loafing.
34. **The bystander effect in shared queues.** A meta-analysis (over 7,700 participants): the presence of others reduced helping (g = -0.35), less so in clear emergencies and among people who weren't strangers. Meta's experiment and Microsoft's, as in strand A. Sources: [Fischer et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21534650/); [Rigby et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17169); [Maddila et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12468). Q1, Q6, Q7: route to a named person, not a group, and nudge whoever is blocking.
35. **A second overseer of automation doesn't help by default.** Two-person crews and solo performers "were equally likely" to miss events the automation missed and to follow wrong directives. A review: automation bias "cannot be prevented by training or instructions, and can affect decision making in individuals as well as in teams". Sources: [Skitka et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11543300/); [Mosier et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327108IJAP1101_1); [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21077562/). Experiments and a review. Q2, Q4: adding a second person who sees the agent's pick doesn't fix deference to the agent.
36. **What reduces automation bias.** A systematic review of 74 studies lists "emphasizing user accountability", where advice sits on the screen, and giving "information versus recommendation". Pilots who felt personally accountable checked automation more. Sources: [Goddard et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21685142/); [Lyell and Coiera 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27516495/); [Mosier et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11540946/). Q2, Q6: named individual accountability and neutral presentation help; keep each person's load low.
37. **Deciding before seeing the machine's answer.** Forcing people to engage before seeing the AI "significantly reduced overreliance" (N = 199), and they liked those designs least. Among 19 veterinary radiologists, those who recorded a provisional answer first agreed with the AI less "regardless of whether the advice is accurate", and sought a colleague's second opinion less. Sources: [Buçinca et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692); [Fogliato et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09696). Experiments, the second small. Q4: it supports the blind first choice, and warns that committed reviewers also discount correct advice, and ask colleagues less.
38. **A second opinion beside the AI's advice.** In three pre-registered experiments, always showing a second opinion with the AI's recommendation reduced over-reliance and increased under-reliance, whether it came from a peer or another model. Letting the person choose when to ask for a peer's opinion could reduce over-reliance without the added under-reliance "in some cases". Source: [Lu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07058). Randomised experiments. Q3, Q4: a colleague's differing blind answer, shown at the reveal, is a counterweight to the agent's case; asking a colleague on demand may be better than always showing.
39. **Advice is normally discounted:** people weight their own opinion above advice, having their own reasons and not the adviser's. Source: [Yaniv and Kleinberger 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11056071/). Four lab studies. Q4, Q8. The strand's inference: this project's 87% move to the agent after the reveal runs against that norm, so either the reveal supplies real reasons the reviewer lacked, or it's deference; two blind human answers on the same card would help tell which.

## What the evidence supports

**When one decider is better than several.** Every framework read names one decider or one judge. One is better when the decision can be undone (4) and when speed matters. A named individual beats a group in the field: 11.6% less time in review (34). An experienced single reviewer comes close to a pair (24). Unequal pairs can do worse than their better member (22).

**When two independent answers are worth their cost.** When a miss is costly and hard to undo: a second independent answer cuts misses from roughly 8 to 13% to 0 to 3% (24). Only if the second answer is truly independent: primed or unblinded checks showed no benefit, or less effort (30, 31). Only if the rule for combining them is fixed in advance (3, 23). The costs are time and more false alarms. The benefit isn't established everywhere (26, 30); doubling selectively, by risk, is what the evidence favours (26, 29).

**Whether later reviewers should see earlier answers.** Not before committing their own (16, 17, 27, 30, 31). After each has committed, showing them is needed to resolve the difference, and didn't produce herding in the one trial that tested it (18).

**How disagreement is best ended.** By a procedure set beforehand: first a quick reconciliation between the two, since most differences are simple errors (23); then a named, qualified third person, or the single decider (3, 28). Not by counting heads (8), and not by confidence (22). The dismissed view is kept with a reason, and the original independent answers are kept (8, 11, 14, 23). An appeal has a ladder and a time limit (8). Discussion closes a disagreement; it doesn't make the result more reliable (19).

**What keeps a shared queue from being nobody's.** A named individual per item, with the group removed from the request (13, 34). Contributions visible and evaluated as each person's (33). A stated response time, falling through to a named second person (9, 15). Reminders aimed at whoever is blocking (34). Caps on load, and pruning of owners who don't respond (12, 13, 15). Possibly not telling a reviewer that someone else holds the same card (31).

## Tensions

- **Independence against communication.** Two heads win only when they can share confidence (22); seeing others degrades independence (17, 27). They fit together only as "independent first, then talk".
- **Does double reading work?** The older, larger review is positive; the digital-era review finds no significant gain and more cost.
- **Double-checking is mandated widely on thin evidence,** and in practice was independent 1% of the time.
- **Herding.** Lab and screening data show conformity; the conference trial found none. The settings differ in whether people had committed first.
- **Can one reasoned objection block?** Yes for Apache's code changes, Gerrit's -2 and consent. No under RAPID, the advice process and rough consensus, where a decider or chair may proceed, with reasons.
- **Deciding first** cuts over-reliance, and also cuts acceptance of correct advice, lowers asking for second opinions, and is disliked (37, 38).
- **Second overseers** don't help when both see the automation's output (35); blinded checks in sequence do (31). **No study was found of two overseers each blind to the automated proposal and to each other, which is the arrangement Baesian would create.**
- **Expert or not.** Cochrane says a non-expert second screener may be an advantage; less experienced or unequal partners do worse elsewhere (22, 24).
- **Two-person rules** are a control against a lone insider, not evidence of better judgment.
- **Frameworks against evidence.** The decision-rights frameworks are normative. No comparative study was found showing that any of them improves decisions.

## Not verified

- **Noise:** the book wasn't read; the 55% figure and the list of remedies rest on Wikipedia's summary.
- **Diffusion of responsibility in email** (Barron and Yechiam 2002; Markey 2000): secondary sources say a request to one addressee got more, and more helpful, replies; the abstracts couldn't be read.
- **Taylor-Phillips and Stinton 2020:** abstract only, so nothing on how each rule for combining two readers performs.
- **Gartlehner's rule for dual screening** isn't stated in the abstract; the lower specificity is consistent with "either reviewer includes".
- **RACI:** no primary source.
- **Classic sociocracy:** finding 7 uses Sociocracy 3.0, a different codification.
- **NIST SP 800-53:** read through a third party's mirror.
- **Finance:** no primary normative source for the four-eyes principle was found.
