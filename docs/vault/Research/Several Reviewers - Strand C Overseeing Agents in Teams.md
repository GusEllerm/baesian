# Several reviewers, strand C: how teams oversee agents and automated changes

Provenance: one of three research strands run on 2026-11-11 for [[Several Reviewers]] (P20), by an agent working read-only from the web; the synthesis is [[Several Reviewers - Research]]. The questions it answers are that note's Q1 to Q8. Pages were fetched on 2026-11-11; "read" means the primary page or the paper's text was fetched, and anything weaker is said. **Quoted wording came through a fetch that summarises pages, so check it against the page before repeating it anywhere public.**

## Findings

### A. Approval products and practices

1. **GitHub Environments: any one of up to six.** "You can list up to six users or teams as reviewers"; "only one of the required reviewers needs to approve the job for it to proceed". An optional "prevent self-review"; admins can bypass by default; a rejection fails the workflow. Sources: [Deployments and environments](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/workflows-and-actions/deployments-and-environments), [Reviewing deployments](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/how-tos/deploy/configure-and-manage-deployments/review-deployments). Read. Q1, Q2, Q5, Q6. The mainstream default is a short named list with the first answer deciding, and self-approval is a separate switch.
2. **GitLab's deployment approvals: a quorum per rule, no timeout.** "Deployments are blocked until all required approvals are given"; "a user can give only one approval per deployment, even if the user is a member of multiple approver groups"; whoever triggered the pipeline can't approve, by default. If the sole approver is deleted, the rule passes to Maintainers, with an audit event. Source: [Deployment approvals](https://docs.gitlab.com/ci/environments/deployment_approvals/). Read. Q2, Q3, Q5, Q6. One person wearing two hats counts once; an approver who vanishes needs a defined fallback.
3. **Azure Pipelines: checks owned outside the pipeline, and a timeout becomes a decision.** "Users modifying the pipeline yaml file can't modify the checks." "If a group is designated as an approver, only one user within the group needs to approve." On timeout the stage is skipped, or "the deployment is rejected". "The list of users who can review an Approval is fixed at the time approvals & checks start running." "A single final negative decision causes the pipeline to be denied." Sources: [Approvals and checks](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/devops/pipelines/process/approvals), [Release approvals](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/devops/pipelines/release/approvals/approvals). Read. Q2, Q3, Q5, Q6. Who may approve is fixed when the request is made; one "no" is final; silence resolves to "no".
4. **AWS CodePipeline: broadcast, then fail after seven days.** Anyone with the permission can approve; the notice goes to every subscriber of a topic; "if no response is submitted within seven days, the action is marked as 'Failed.'" Source: [Manual approvals](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/codepipeline/latest/userguide/approvals.html). Read. Q1, Q6, Q7.
5. **Power Automate names the standard rules.** "First to respond": "approval or rejection by any approver completes the request". "Everyone must approve": actions run "after all the approvers respond, or when a single rejection occurs". Also approval in sequence. Source: [Get started with approvals](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/get-started-approvals). Read. Q2, Q3, Q7. The vocabulary is first responder, unanimity with one veto, and sequence; none covers two people giving different positive answers.
6. **Slack's workflow buttons:** "the workflow won't proceed until someone clicks the button"; clicking can be restricted to certain people; a "single-click" setting disables the button once one person has clicked it. No timeout. Source: [Build a workflow](https://slack.com/help/articles/17542172840595-Build-a-workflow--Create-a-workflow-in-Slack). Read. Q2, Q6, Q7.
7. **Agent approval layers have no notion of who answers.** None of these defines routing among people, a quorum, escalation or clearing an item for the others. LangChain's middleware: approve, edit, reject or respond per tool call; "an interrupt you do not answer stays pending". LangGraph's Agent Inbox: its settings kept "in your browser's local storage", no users or assignment, and its repository archived. OpenAI's Agents SDK: pending approvals returned as `interruptions`; the app must authenticate the reviewer ("a run ID or decision ID is not proof of authorization"); no timeouts. Claude's Agent SDK: "the callback can stay pending indefinitely". Sources: [LangChain](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langchain/human-in-the-loop), [Agent Inbox](https://github.com/langchain-ai/agent-inbox), [OpenAI](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-js/guides/human-in-the-loop/), [Claude](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/user-input). Read. Q1, Q2, Q6, Q7. Several reviewers is open space in agent tooling, and the default failure is an indefinite wait.
8. **gotoHuman assigns, and doesn't say how several answers resolve.** A request can go "only to selected users of your team"; otherwise it "defaults to all users or the user who triggered the workflow". Source: [Send requests](https://docs.gotohuman.com/send-requests). Read in part. Q1, Q7.
9. **LangSmith's annotation queues: a quorum, claims that expire, blind reviewers.** "Number of reviewers per run" sets how many must mark an item done before it leaves the queue. "Reserving an item locks it for your review for a set amount of time"; an abandoned reservation expires and the item "is then released back into the queue". "Reviewers cannot view the feedback left by other reviewers", while "comments on items are visible to all reviewers". Source: [Annotation queues](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/annotation-queues). Read. Q2, Q4, Q6, Q7. The closest existing precedent for blind answers from several reviewers with a time-boxed claim, though for annotation, not gating.
10. **Coding agents' policy is layered: the organisation sets ceilings, individuals tighten or pick defaults.** Claude Code: rules are evaluated deny, then ask, then allow; managed settings are highest, and "if a tool is denied at any level, no other level can allow it"; "don't ask again" is stored per repository. Codex: an admin's `requirements.toml` constrains approval policy and sandbox mode. Cursor: "team settings take precedence over individual and project configuration". Factory: for hard controls "the highest level present wins"; for session defaults "the most-local level wins". Devin: a team's lists "cannot be overridden by individual users". Sources: [Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permissions), [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/enterprise/managed-configuration.md), [Cursor](https://cursor.com/docs/agent/security/run-modes), [Factory](https://docs.factory.com/enterprise/hierarchical-settings-and-org-control.md), [Devin](https://docs.devin.ai/cli/enterprise/team-settings). Read. Q5, Q8. Five vendors converge on "the admin sets limits, a person adjusts within them", and on keeping enforced requirements apart from defaults a person may change. Learned allowances are per person or per repo, never silently the team's.
11. **GitHub's Copilot cloud agent: whoever asked can't approve their agent's work.** The platform "prevents the user who asked Copilot cloud agent to create a pull request from approving it"; the agent "cannot approve or merge a pull request". Source: [Risks and mitigations](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/coding-agent/risks-and-mitigations). Read. Q2, Q5. With several reviewers, "the person who launched the agent may not be the one who answers its card" becomes a rule that's available.
12. **Approval by an outside body: slower, not safer.** DORA 2019: respondents "were 2.6 times more likely to be low performers" where an external body such as a change advisory board approved changes; "we found no evidence" that formal approval lowered the rate of failed changes; a change process people clearly understood made teams "1.8 times more likely" to be elite. DORA recommends peer review within the team. Sources: [Accelerate State of DevOps 2019](https://dora.dev/publications/pdf/state-of-devops-2019.pdf), read; [Streamlining change approval](https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/). A large practitioner survey, correlational. Q1, Q2, Q6. Sending cards to a distant committee is the option with the least support; the nearest competent person, and a process the agent can predict, are what go with good outcomes.
13. **Code review tools give one objector a block,** with a way to dismiss it, and separate "I looked" from "I decide" (Gerrit's +1 against +2). As in [[Several Reviewers - Strand A Review Tools]]. Q2, Q3.

### B. Routing by expertise and ownership

14. **CODEOWNERS routes by path, any one owner suffices, and stale entries fail open.** GitHub: an invalid line "will be skipped"; an owner who doesn't exist or lacks access means "a code owner will not be assigned". GitLab: an entry with no valid owner "can never be satisfied, GitLab auto-approves it". Sources: [GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-code-owners), [GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/codeowners/), [GitLab, advanced](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/codeowners/advanced/). Read. Q1, Q5, Q6. A map of routes needs an explicit catch-all and an explicit rule for a dead route. Tools detect only an unknown user, never "the wrong person now".
15. **GitHub resolves a team request into named people,** as in strand A. Q1, Q6, Q7.
16. **Service catalogues insist on one owner.** Backstage: `spec.owner` is required; the owner is "the singular entity (commonly a team) that bears ultimate responsibility"; the field is "not to be used by automated processes to for example assign authorization". Source: [Descriptor format](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/descriptor-format/). Read. Q1, Q5. Ownership and permission to decide are kept as separate things.
17. **On-call escalation is the mature answer to "nobody answered".** PagerDuty: a default timeout of 30 minutes; all targets on a rule are notified at once; a rule with nobody on call is skipped; "once a user acknowledges the incident, the escalation policy stops escalating and PagerDuty stops sending notifications"; an optional acknowledgement timeout re-triggers an incident that was acknowledged and not finished. Opsgenie: rules fire on "not acknowledged" or "not closed". Sources: [PagerDuty escalation policies](https://docs.pagerduty.com/incident-management/schedules/escalation-policies), [service settings](https://docs.pagerduty.com/incident-management/services/configurable-service-settings); [Opsgenie escalations](https://support.atlassian.com/opsgenie/docs/how-do-escalations-work-in-opsgenie/). Read. Q6, Q7. A ladder (one person, then the next, then everyone), with one person's acknowledgement silencing the rest, and a guard for "claimed but not finished".
18. **Linear: one assignee, and triage duty as notifying or assigning.** Delegating to an agent keeps a human assignee: "the assignee remains responsible for the work". Sources: [Triage](https://linear.app/docs/triage), [Assigning issues](https://linear.app/docs/assigning-issues). Read. Q1, Q2, Q6.
19. **Ownership goes stale through reorganisation.** "Who is the most suitable owner of a given asset changes over time ... due to reorganization and individual function changes"; Facebook built a system that mines activity to suggest owners. Source: Ahlgren et al., [Ownership at Large](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07352), ICPC 2020, abstract read. Q1, Q8.

### C. Research on oversight at team scale

20. **A request addressed to several people gets fewer answers.** A field experiment with 400 graduate students: 38% replied when addressed alone, 33% with one other, 14% with 14 others, 14% with 49 others. Source: Blair, Thompson and Wuensch 2005, [Electronic helping behavior](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Blair-Thompson-Wuensch-2005.pdf); the rates were read from the paper's table by the fetch tool. Q1, Q6, Q7. "Send it to everyone" has a measured cost, most of it there by a handful of recipients.
21. **Naming one responsible reviewer speeds review:** Meta's experiment, as in strand A; the authors conclude "the bystander effect is real". Q1, Q6. One named person beats a group even when picked at random among plausible candidates.
22. **Telling the person who is blocking that it's their turn clears stalled items:** Microsoft's Nudge, as in strand A. Q6, Q7.
23. **Google runs on one reviewer,** as in strand A; new members are added as reviewers on purpose, since routing by history can't see them. Q1, Q2, Q6, Q8.
24. **Participation, not the existence of an approval, tracks quality** (McIntosh et al., as in strand A). Q2.
25. **Reviewers disagree often** (Hirao et al., as in strand A): plan for a quarter or so of items with several answers to conflict; seeing an earlier approval didn't stop later reviewers dissenting. Q3, Q4.
26. **Visible earlier comments focus attention narrowly** (Spadini et al., as in strand A). Q4.
27. **Visible earlier verdicts cause herding toward approval.** A randomised experiment on a news site: a prior positive rating "increased the likelihood of positive ratings by 32%" and raised final ratings "by 25% on average"; a negative one was corrected by later raters. Source: [Muchnik, Aral and Taylor, Science 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23929980/). A large randomised field experiment. Q4. With 9 and 26: show a second reviewer the first's reasons or concerns, if anything, not the first's verdict, before they choose.
28. **Discussion makes a group agree with itself, not with other groups** (Pier et al. 2017, as in strand B). Q3, Q4.
29. **A second monitor doesn't reduce over-reliance on automation** (Skitka et al. 2000, as in strand B). Q2, Q4, Q6. A second approver given the same view of an agent's proposal shouldn't be assumed to add scrutiny.
30. **Felt personal accountability reduces automation bias; training alone doesn't** (Mosier et al.; Goddard et al.; Parasuraman and Manzey, as in strand B). Q2, Q4, Q5. Support for one named decider per card, and for choosing before seeing the agent's recommendation.
31. **Double checking in medicine: weak evidence, variable compliance** (Koyama et al. 2020, as in strand B). Q2, Q4.
32. **More alerts, fewer heeded.** Among 112 primary-care clinicians, "likelihood of reminder acceptance dropped by 30% for each additional reminder received per encounter". Separately, "drug safety alerts are overridden by clinicians in 49% to 96% of cases". Sources: [Ancker et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5387195/); van der Sijs et al., JAMIA 2006. A retrospective cohort; a literature review. Q1, Q6, Q7. Volume and repeats per person drive the drop, and sending to N people multiplies both.
33. **A prompt's design changes whether people stop.** Over 25 million browser warnings, users clicked through "70.2% of Google Chrome's SSL warnings" against a third of Firefox's. For Android's install permissions, "17% of people paid attention". Sources: [Akhawe and Felt 2013](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity13/technical-sessions/presentation/akhawe); [Felt et al. 2012](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2012/EECS-2012-26.html). Field telemetry; a survey and lab study. Q6, Q7.
34. **Coding agents' approval prompts are mostly accepted, and autonomy grows with experience.** Anthropic: "users accept 93% of them anyway", which "leads to approval fatigue". Full auto-approval rises from about 20% of sessions for new users to over 40% by 750 sessions. Sources: [Claude Code auto mode](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode), [Measuring agent autonomy](https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy). A vendor's telemetry. Q6, Q8. Trust moves with exposure, person by person: a new reviewer and a veteran will want different gates.
35. **Agents' pull requests are often merged unchanged, and reviewers approve more over time.** Of 567 Claude Code pull requests in 157 projects, 83.8% were merged, 54.9% of those "without further modification". Among 400 repeat reviewers, approval of agents' pull requests rose "from 30.1% to 36.8%" with experience, and inline comments fell 22%; the authors read it as "reflexive habituation under growing workload". Sources: [Watanabe et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14745); [Yu et al., Habituation at the Gate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22721). Repository mining, abstracts; the habituation reading is the authors', not causal. Q6, Q8.
36. **More points of oversight don't mean better intervention.** 48 participants, 192 live sessions with computer-use agents: the oversight strategy "affected exposure to problematic actions but not users' ability to intervene"; users "judged whether the agent acted correctly, not whether the action was safe"; "upfront approval inhibited runtime scrutiny". Separately, on 125 hand-labelled agent actions, "reviewers only moderately agree on what is risky (Fleiss' kappa = 0.52)". Sources: [Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04918); [Turan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08919). Preprints, abstracts. Q3, Q4, Q6. Two competent reviewers will label the same agent action differently fairly often: disagreement is a base rate.
37. **Many hands.** Thompson: when many officials contribute to a decision, "it is difficult even in principle to identify who is morally responsible"; he argues for personal over collective responsibility. Source: Thompson, APSR 74(4), 1980. Conceptual. Q2, Q5.

### D. Inbox semantics for an item several people receive

38. **Gerrit makes "whose turn" explicit state,** as in strand A. Q6, Q7. Who received it and who is expected to act now are two sets, and the second drives badges and notifications.
39. **One assignee by design, others as followers.** Asana: one assignee "ensures that there's never a sense of 'who's responsible'", after Apple's directly responsible individual; others are collaborators. Linear: one assignee. GitHub is the exception, with up to ten. Sources: [Asana](https://asana.com/resources/why-one-assignee), [Linear](https://linear.app/docs/assigning-issues). Documented behaviour and a vendor's rationale. Q1, Q2, Q7.
40. **Shared inboxes use claim, presence and resolved states; plain notifications stay per person.** Google Groups' collaborative inbox: "Take" assigns to yourself, "Drop" unassigns, with resolutions Complete, No action needed, Duplicate. Help Scout: "a yellow indicator lets you know another user already has a conversation open. It will turn red when the user is actively typing." Zendesk: presence isn't registered "when the screen isn't active", or across several devices. GitHub's notifications carry a reason (assigned, team mention, review requested), and "Done" clears only your own inbox. Sources: [Google Groups](https://support.google.com/a/users/answer/167430), [Help Scout](https://docs.helpscout.com/article/1583-collaborate-with-your-team-in-the-inbox), [Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4408825035674), [GitHub notifications](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/managing-subscriptions-and-notifications-on-github/setting-up-notifications/about-notifications). Read. Q7.

## Patterns that recur

- **Any one of N decides, as the default:** GitHub Environments, Azure's approver groups, CODEOWNERS, Power Automate's "first to respond", Slack's single click, PagerDuty's acknowledge, AWS CodePipeline, Google's code review.
- **A quorum or unanimity as an option:** GitLab's deployment rules and code owner sections, GitHub's required approval count, Power Automate's "everyone must approve", LangSmith's reviewers per run.
- **One negative is a veto, with a way out:** Power Automate, Azure, Gerrit's -2, GitHub's request for changes.
- **No approving your own:** GitHub Environments, GitLab, Azure, GitHub's last-pusher rule, the Copilot cloud agent.
- **A group address is resolved to named people:** GitHub's team review assignment, Google's round robin, Meta's experiment, Linear's triage responsibility, PagerDuty's schedules.
- **A timed ladder for no response:** PagerDuty, Opsgenie. Deployment tools make a timeout a refusal. Agent frameworks wait forever.
- **A claim, a lock, or presence:** Google Groups' Take and Drop, LangSmith's reservations that expire, Help Scout's and Zendesk's indicators, PagerDuty's acknowledge.
- **"Whose turn" as state of its own:** Gerrit's attention set, Microsoft's Nudge.
- **Policy above the individual; individuals only tighten, or pick defaults:** Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Factory, Devin; Azure's checks; GitHub's environment and branch rules.
- **One accountable owner:** Asana, Linear, Backstage.
- **Hiding other reviewers' input:** only LangSmith, among the tools read.

## Failure modes worth designing against

1. **A bystander stall** when a card goes to a group (20, 21, 39).
2. **A dead route failing open** (an owner rule that can't be met auto-approved, invalid lines skipped, an empty on-call rule skipped: 14, 17), **or failing closed for ever,** as agent frameworks do (7).
3. **Silence turned into a decision without anyone choosing:** skipped, rejected or failed on timeout (1, 3, 4).
4. **Rubber-stamping that grows with volume and familiarity** (32, 33, 34, 35).
5. **A second approver who adds delay and no scrutiny,** above all one looking at the same recommendation (12, 29, 31).
6. **Herding once an earlier verdict is visible** (27, 28).
7. **Claimed and never finished:** needs an expiry, or a re-trigger (9, 17).
8. **The set of approvers changing under an open request** (2, 3).
9. **Approving your own, through the agent you launched** (11).
10. **Load piling on the best-matched person;** rotation that ignores workload (18, 21).
11. **Treating disagreement as exceptional,** when it runs at 15% to 37% of patches scored more than once, and kappa 0.52 on labels of risk (25, 36).
12. **Approval given early suppressing later scrutiny;** judging "is it right" and not "is it safe" (36).
13. **Notifications that stay live for everyone after one person has acted** (4, 40).
14. **Presence indicators that miss people,** on inactive screens or several devices (40).
15. **Diffuse accountability when several can approve** (30, 37).

## Gaps: what nobody seems to have built or studied

- **No agent approval layer defines routing among several people, a quorum, escalation, or clearing an item for the others** (7, 8).
- **No tool combines blind independent answers with a rule for resolving them, as a gate.** LangSmith hides feedback, for annotation; code review is fully visible.
- **No study was found of several people overseeing the same LLM agent.** The work of 2025 and 2026 is single-user studies or mining pull requests; nothing measures diffusion of responsibility, or what a second approver is worth, for agents' decisions.
- **Routing is by artefact** (path, service, environment), **not by kind of decision or domain expertise.** No "decision ownership" map (a principal investigator, a statistician, a data steward) with precedence and a catch-all was found.
- **No tool varies who is asked, or how many must answer, by each item's stakes;** requirements attach to the resource.
- **Staleness is detected only as an unknown user.**
- **No evidence on which scope for learned rules (person, repo, team) cuts prompts without lowering scrutiny.**
- **Onboarding approvers is barely documented:** only Google's practice of adding new members as extra reviewers.
- **No guidance on how long a waiting agent should wait,** as opposed to a waiting deployment or page.
- **"Not my area" among several reviewers:** only reassigning and escalation exist; no data on loops.

## Not verified

- **HumanLayer:** every SDK documentation address returned 404, and its README now describes another product; search snippets mention restricting who may respond, and timed fallbacks. Not confirmed.
- **Spinnaker's manual judgment:** role restriction comes from third parties' pages; no timeout found.
- **GitHub's 30-day limit on an environment's approval:** seen only in Enterprise Server's pages.
- **Barron and Yechiam 2002:** reported only as Blair et al. describe it. **Blair et al.'s rates** were read from the PDF's table by the fetch tool, and are consistent with the stated 99 of 400.
- **Hirao et al., Schickora, Pier et al.:** abstracts or search summaries only.
- **Front, OpsLevel, Zendesk's main collision article, Jira Service Management's declines:** search snippets or partial pages only.
- **The 2026 preprints** (Chen et al., Turan, Yu et al.) were read as abstracts; whether they've been peer reviewed isn't known.
